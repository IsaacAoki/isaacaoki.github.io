MScR Thesis · 2023 · University of Bristol

PRMT5 inhibition in Wilms’ tumour

Wilms’ Tumour is the most common paediatric renal malignancy that accounts for 85% of all paediatric renal tumours. MYCN has been documented to be overexpressed in Wilms’ Tumour and mutations such as copy number gain are associated with a high relapse rate in high-risk blastemal Wilms’ Tumour. However, due to MYCN’s undruggable structure and its complex protein-protein dynamics, attempts to target MYCN have been a challenge. Here, both depletion via small interfering RNA and inhibition via small molecule inhibitor of protein arginine methyltransferase 5 (PRMT5) led to impaired growth and increased apoptosis in Wilms’ Tumour cell lines, WiT49 and 17.94. Inhibition of PRMT5 activity using GSK591 has also resulted in decreased MYCN protein and transcript levels. Additionally, PRMT5 inhibition has also led to a reduction in MYCN targets such as EZH2 at the protein and RNA levels in both WiT49 and 17.94 cells. These results suggest that PRMT5 may be able to modulate the expression of both MYCN and MYCN targets, indicating that a PRMT5/MYCN/EZH2 oncogenic axis might contribute to Wilms’ Tumour.

Furthermore, quantitative gene transcription analysis and immunoblotting of the WiT49 cell line suggested that inhibition of PRMT5 using GSK591 has also led to the re-expression of anti-apoptotic protein BCL2. This was not observed with the 17.94 cell line, suggesting that GSK591 may have variable effects depending on the cell lines used. Interestingly, a combination treatment targeting both PRMT5 and BCL2 have worked synergistically to induce apoptosis in both Wilms’ Tumour cell lines. This finding is promising as it may serve as a novel approach in treating Wilms’ Tumour that is heterogeneous in nature.

Together, this study has shown that PRMT5 may be a therapeutic target in Wilms’ Tumour and a combination treatment targeting both PRMT5 and BCL2 protein using small molecule inhibitors could represent a promising treatment method for Wilms’ Tumour.

BSc dissertation · 2020 · University of the West of England

Investigation of thrombin inhibition using D-dimer

D-dimer is a soluble protein fragment that results from the degradation of a fibrin clot through an enzymatic process called fibrinolysis. Various research papers have proved the characterisation of D-dimer as an essential biomarker in detecting the presence of fibrinolysis and intravascular coagulation. Subsequently, D-dimer has been a target for extensive research in being used as an indicative measure for diagnosing patients with venous thromboembolic (VTE) diseases and has been used clinically through an immuno-based D-dimer assay. However, measuring D-dimer levels using immunoassays are costly and demands professional training for usage. Thus, the Yumizen G200 was tested for its potential to serve as an alternative for diagnosing VTE diseases.

Thrombin time measurements were made using the Yumizen G200 with the TT assay kit. Thrombin concentrations (3.33–16.65 NIH/mL) and calculated fibrinogen concentrations (190.67–76.27 µg/mL) were evaluated and optimised to analyse their effect on TT assay. Volume of D-dimer was determined and assayed with 3.33 NIH/mL of thrombin and 76.27 µg/mL of calculated fibrinogen concentration to analyse the presence of inhibitory effects of D-dimer against thrombin.

Active thrombin activity is inhibited by D-dimer in the presence of fibrinogen. Clauss Fibrinogen assay displayed the threshold limit of the Yumizen G200 at 50% plasma fibrinogen dilution. TT test confirmed the lowest concentration of thrombin (3.33 NIH/mL) was able to generate a reading and can be used for D-dimer testing. Optimisation of TT assay identified the threshold limit (3.33 NIH/mL of thrombin and 76.27 µg/mL of calculated fibrinogen concentration) to further test for D-dimer inhibition.

The Yumizen G200 was able to detect the elevation of D-dimer levels, indicating its potentiality in being used as an alternative to immunoassays in point-of-care testing for VTE derived conditions in areas without equipped laboratories and trained personnel.

Written task · Ogilvy Health

If you could cure any disease, which disease would you choose, and why?

You’re walking home from the grocery store. Same route, same playlist in your ear. Then your foot catches on a pavement you’ve walked a thousand times and you’re on the ground. You pick yourself up and keep walking. In the shower that night, a bruise blooms across the right side of your face. Your hands are fine. You brush it off. The next morning, you reach for the salt and your fingers close on nothing. You try again. This time you grip it. Without deciding to, you let go. The container hits the floor. You go on with your day.

Over the next few weeks, the incidents become harder to ignore. Mugs slip from your hands. You stumble more often. You begin to feel unfamiliar in your own body. One Sunday evening, you count: six broken mugs and three falls in a week. You decide to write it all down. By the time you finish, you notice your handwriting has changed, the letters slant and no longer sit on the lines. You flip back through older shopping lists. The difference is unmistakable.

What follows is months of appointments. A neurologist examines you limb by limb. Then an MRI. Then an EMG. Then more. Each test comes back telling you what it isn’t, never what it is. Eventually, you sit in a neurologist’s office. Amyotrophic lateral sclerosis. ALS. It is a disease of the motor neurons, the nerve cells that carry signals from brain to muscle. The disease is progressive. Over time, you will lose control of your body. Your memory, your awareness, your sense of who you are will remain fully intact throughout. The doctor says these things kindly. They are the careful clinical words for what, in simpler terms, has only one meaning: you will be present for every step of your decline.

I do not have ALS, and I have never lost someone close to me to it. What draws me to this disease is the gap between what we can describe, what we can explain, and what we can treat. We have studied ALS for over a century, raised hundreds of millions of dollars to fight, and still cannot diagnose it early or meaningfully treat it. In the UK alone, around 5,000 people are living with ALS, with 1,700 newly diagnosed each year.1 Recent modelling from King’s College London projects that figure will rise to more than 2,600 by 2116, not because ALS is becoming more common, but because we are growing older.1

Part of what makes ALS so hard to catch is the variability of the disease. Symptoms can begin anywhere in the body. In about 70% of cases, the first signs appear in the limb muscles: a hand that begins dropping objects, or a foot that trips a little too often. The remaining 25–30% present with a bulbar onset: a word that comes out slurred at dinner, choking at meals, a voice that has begun to sound hoarse without explanation.2,3 In some patients, ALS reaches beyond motor function into behavioural changes, language problems, and impairment in executive function.4 Around 15% of cases, these manifestations are severe enough to meet the clinical criteria for frontotemporal dementia (FTD).4

ALS and FTD share a common driver, the C9orf72 repeat expansion, and the same TDP-43 protein aggregates in many cases.2,4 In most patients, the downstream cascade plays out. Whether triggered by a known genetic mutation or an unknown sporadic cause, the protein TDP-43 mislocalises and aggregates inside motor neurons. This disrupts glutamate regulation, causing the neurotransmitter to accumulate at the synapse to toxic levels, driving excitotoxic motor neuron death.2 The dropping, the falling, the slanting letters follow.

This discovery underpins the worldwide approval for Riluzole, a first-line disease modifying therapy that blocks glutamatergic transmission.5 In randomised trials, Riluzole extends survival by 2–6 months; however, real-world data suggest the benefit may be greater.2,5,6 Other approved treatments include Edaravone, which may slow functional decline in a selected subgroup of patients, and Tofersen, the first gene-directed therapy that targets SOD1 protein production but is indicated only for patients carrying a SOD1 mutation.2,7

Curing ALS would mean more than extending survival; it would restore life, and the freedom to live without fearing what comes next. A disease this heterogeneous cannot be solved without unlocking how the brain truly works. Solving ALS would be proof that we can intervene in neurological disease, laying the foundation for tackling diseases like FTD, Parkinson’s, and Alzheimer’s.
